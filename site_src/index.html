Where the lights go out and the internet goes dark.

Internet outages measured by IODA and Cloudflare Radar, censorship measured by OONI, BGP hijacks and route leaks, power at the Zaporizhzhya nuclear plant and on the US grid, submarine cable corridors, and what the big cloud and satellite providers report about themselves.

How to read the map

Shaded: a country with an IODA outage. Dashed outline: OONI at or above 15%. Blue dots: Cloudflare Radar items. Yellow dots: power (the Zaporizhzhya NPP, a US balancing authority under forecast at a point Blackout placed in its area, a US state over 50,000 meters out, a city darker at night); small yellow dots: US counties with meters out at ODIN's reporting utilities, larger for more. Dark dots: widely seen BGP hijacks and route leaks, at the country of the networks involved. Gold lines: cable corridors drawn schematically, each landing station joined to the corridor's reference point; they are not cable routes.

Now

    Outages measured by IODA

    Countries where an IODA signal fell 25% or more for at least 30 minutes; a dip in probing or darknet alone while BGP held is marked single-source.

    How this is measured

    Median during the event against the median of the same hours of the 7 days before it, per IODA signal (BGP, active probing, darknet). A signal whose baseline median is under 20 (too few counts to measure a drop on) is left out and named. BO-IND-1: BGP or active probing ≥25% for ≥30 min. BO-IND-4: one signal drops while BGP holds, listed as a single-signal dip. For each BO-IND-1 outage Blackout reads OONI in that country for the outage's own hours, against the same hours of the 5 days before: when blocking reaches BO-IND-3's line in those hours and at least doubles, the item supports a shutdown (BO-H-1A), still with no probability until Verdict has scored the rule. Otherwise a drop fits a power or physical fault as well, and the item says what OONI showed, including when it was not read. IODA's own event score is a magnitude, never a percentage. A signal that can't be read is shown as not measured; nothing is filled in from another signal.

    Cloudflare Radar

    Outages Cloudflare's analysts list, with the cause Cloudflare states, and traffic anomalies Cloudflare verified. Reported by Cloudflare, never assessed here.

    How this is measured

    Outages Cloudflare's analysts list in the Radar Outage Center, with the cause and type Cloudflare states (BO-IND-9), and traffic anomalies Cloudflare's own detection flagged and Cloudflare verified, which come with no cause (BO-IND-10). Ongoing, or ended within 72 h. Cloudflare sees its own traffic, so this is a line of evidence separate from IODA. Reported by Cloudflare, never assessed here. Data CC BY-NC 4.0.

    NetBlocks

    Disruptions NetBlocks, an internet observatory, reports from its own measurements, often partial and regional ones that country-wide signals miss. Quoted with a link to each post, never assessed here.

    How this is read

    Every post on NetBlocks' public Bluesky account is read each run (BO-IND-20). Posts of the last 72 h that name a country, a platform or a network are listed. Places, causes, services and any level NetBlocks states ("67% of ordinary levels") are parsed from its words and can miss a name. NetBlocks chooses what it posts: no post is not evidence of no disruption. NetBlocks' posts are its copyright (all rights reserved), so Blackout quotes only their opening words, only while a post is listed here, and links each one. Every post since April 2023 is in data/netblocks.json as its time, link and parsed fields.

    Censorship measured by OONI

    Countries where 15% or more of OONI's measurements show confirmed blocking or anomalies.

    How this is measured

    Confirmed blocking + anomaly share of all OONI measurements over 7 days, all tests together (BO-IND-3: ≥15% over ≥50 measurements). A fixed threshold, not a change from baseline: some countries sit above it every day, so these items are routine and are never read as evidence about a particular outage; that comes from OONI in the outage's own hours (see Outages).

    Context: shutdown history and what OONI tests

    Access Now's record of shutdowns in the last full year, and Citizen Lab's lists of the sites OONI probes test. Context only; neither changes a judgment.

    How this is used

    Access Now's STOP sheet (BO-EEI-6): the shutdowns Access Now documented in each country in the last full year, with the causes it states. Blackout shows it beside a current outage or OONI row as history; it never raises or lowers a likelihood. Access Now states no licence for the data, so none is claimed. Citizen Lab's URL test lists (BO-EEI-7): how many URLs, in which categories, OONI probes in a country test; a rate over a short list means something different from one over a long list. Data CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

    Zaporizhzhya Nuclear Power Plant: off-site power

    How many off-site power lines the plant has, as the IAEA's newest numbered update states it.

    How this is read

    The update numbers come from the IAEA's Top News feed and the text from the Wayback Machine's first capture of each update (iaea.org refuses scripts, and Blackout doesn't work around it). A sentence naming the plant that says it is without off-site power, or names its single remaining line, sets the state (BO-IND-13; before the war the plant had ten lines). An update that says nothing current about it hands over to the newest one that does; an update more than 10 days old with nothing newer is flagged. Reported by the IAEA and quoted; Blackout does not assess it. IAEA content is reused with acknowledgement and implies no IAEA endorsement.

    US grid: demand against forecast (EIA-930)

    US balancing authorities serving far less than their own day-ahead forecast implies, for 3 hours or more.

    How this is measured

    Each US balancing authority reports to the Energy Information Administration its demand every hour and its forecast for each hour a day ahead (form EIA-930). When a storm or strikes take customers off, their load goes with them. For every balancing authority with a median forecast of 1,000 MW or more, Blackout skips the two newest hours (often partial), takes the 24 before them, and compares each hour's demand with its forecast against that authority's own usual ratio over the 168 hours before (some forecasts run far off every day, which EIA allows). An hour is low when demand is 25% or more under what that ratio implies, or further where an authority's forecasts often miss (one and a half times as far under as its 1st-percentile hour); BO-IND-16 is 3 or more low hours in a row.

    Calibrated on real EIA-930 data: it finds Hurricane Milton at Tampa Electric (74% under) and Duke Energy Florida (52%), Hurricane Helene at Duke Energy Carolinas (32%) and Winter Storm Uri at ERCOT (43%); it misses Hurricane Beryl at ERCOT (22% at worst). From 1 October to 13 November 2026 it gave 2 runs across 38 authorities, neither matched to a reported outage. A gap also fits a forecast miss, batteries charging or a reporting error, and the figures are preliminary, corrected within days. Points on the map are placed by Blackout in each authority's area and are schematic. Source: U.S. Energy Information Administration (public domain).

    US outages by county (ODIN)

    Meters without power by county, as the utilities that report to ODIN state them.

    How this is measured

    ODIN, the Outage Data Initiative Nationwide (Oak Ridge National Laboratory for the US Department of Energy), carries the outage data utilities share in its standard: over 155 utilities and about 47 million customers (February 2026). A county with no row may still be dark, because its utility may not report to ODIN. Counties are placed with the Census Bureau's 2025 Gazetteer. BO-IND-14: a state above 50,000 meters out, the size of loss form DOE-417 makes reportable (more than 50,000 customers for an hour or more); here it is one reading summed over a state, and a meter is close to but not the same as a customer. ODIN states no licence for its public data, so none is claimed.

    Base rates come from EAGLE-I's outage history (Oak Ridge National Laboratory; Brelsford et al., figshare, Creative Commons Attribution 4.0), which reads utilities' public outage maps every 15 minutes and covered about 92% of US customers by 2022. For 2018–2025 Blackout summed each state's counties in every 15-minute reading and counted the times a state stayed over 50,000 customers out for an hour or more (readings no more than an hour apart joined). EAGLE-I reads more utilities than ODIN, so a state over the line at ODIN's utilities alone is rarer still; coverage grew over those years, so the early ones may count fewer. The history is read once, offline, never by the page or the live run.

    Night lights over watched cities (NASA Black Marble)

    Cities much darker at night than on their own recent clear nights.

    How this is measured

    NASA's Black Marble VJ146A2 (VIIRS on NOAA-20), corrected for moonlight and the surface: near real time from NASA LANCE, the standard product from LAADS for older nights. For each city a box about 44 km across; only clear, high-quality pixels; a night counts when 60% of the box is clear. BO-IND-15: the newest counted night, at most 3 days old, 40% or more below the median of at least 5 counted nights in the 14 before, when that median is at least 1.0 nW/cm²/sr. Calibrated on 30 nights: it flags Kyiv after the strike of 8 October 2026 and Khartoum after the drone strike of 27 October 2026. Darker fits a grid failure, strikes or load shedding, and also smoke, dust or snow the cloud mask missed. NASA data carries no restrictions; near-real-time data is provided as is.

    Route hijacks and leaks (Cloudflare Radar)

    BGP origin hijacks and route leaks Cloudflare's detection saw widely in the last 3 days.

    How this is measured

    Cloudflare Radar's hijack detection (confidence 8 and above, Cloudflare's high band) and route-leak detection, every page of both. BO-IND-11: a hijack seen by 25 or more route-collector peers. BO-IND-12: a leak of 100 or more prefixes seen by 25 or more peers, one item per leaking network. Everything else is counted by country below. Routing data alone doesn't tell a deliberate hijack (BO-H-4A) from an operator error (BO-H-4B) or a legitimate change the detector misreads (BO-H-4C). Reported by Cloudflare; Blackout does not assess intent. Radar's event pages refuse scripts, so items link Radar's routing page. Data CC BY-NC 4.0.

    Hijacks counted where the victim networks are; leaks where the leaking network is.

    Cable corridor alerts

    Countries served by the same submarine cable corridor dropping together, or a drop near a suite tip.

    How this is measured

    Warning problem BO-WP-2: triggered when ≥2 countries sharing a submarine corridor drop ≥20% within 2 hours of each other (BO-IND-2), or when a drop co-occurs with a BO-IND-5 tip (≤50 km of a corridor point). Outages in a flagged corridor are published as observations pointing at the corridor item (BO-H-2A), not as shutdown assessments.

    Corridor register

    The five corridors Blackout watches, with their cables and landing stations.

    How this is compiled

    Hand-compiled and checked against TeleGeography's public Submarine Cable Map; no TeleGeography data is copied and no TeleGeography licence is claimed. On the map each corridor is drawn schematically: its landing stations joined to the corridor's reference point. Those lines are not cable routes.

    Suite tips

    Earthquakes and volcanoes from Elements, AIS loitering from Wake, and attacks on telecom carriers from Vigil.

    How this is measured

    Interests BO-INT-1 (earthquakes, volcanoes), BO-INT-2 (AIS loitering or anchor drag; Wake is not live yet) and BO-INT-3 (attacks on telecom carriers). A tip counts toward a cable-fault call (BO-IND-5) only when its own coordinates fall within 50 km of a corridor point; tips naming an outage country are shown as context and do not change any likelihood.

    US flights: FAA ground stops

    Ground stops the FAA issues for every US airport, for a whole airline, or for an equipment or communications outage, as the FAA reports them.

    How this is measured

    Read from the advisories database of the FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center, today and yesterday (UTC), every 4 hours (BO-IND-17). A ground stop counts when it covers every airport, names a whole airline (as on 19 Jul 2024, the CrowdStrike update), is a carrier's request at two or more airports within 2 h (as on 6 Aug 2025, United's own outage), or names an equipment or communications outage at an airport. Weather, volume, staffing, runway and construction stops are left out. Each stays listed for 24 h after it is cancelled or its effective time ends.

    A ground stop is the FAA's tool, so an airline outage that only holds flights at the gate, with no stop requested, does not appear here (United on 16 Nov 2026). Blackout reports what the FAA stopped and the condition it names; it does not assess why a system failed.

    Outages the public and the press report

    US banks, payments, airlines, hotels, carriers, email and government services that public Bluesky posts or news headlines suddenly report down, counted against each one's own usual. Not confirmed by the companies.

    How this is measured

    Public posts (BO-IND-18): for each of 44 watched names, Bluesky's public search counts posts naming it with “down” or with “outage” in the 6 hours to the run, against the same searches over the 7 days before, scaled to 6 hours. A surge is at least 30 posts and at least 5 times the usual. Blackout keeps only the counts: no post, text or author, so a deleted post leaves nothing here. Each row links Bluesky's own search for the words.

    News headlines (BO-IND-19): every 15-minute file of the GDELT Project's Global Knowledge Graph in the same 6 hours, counting articles whose headlines name the service with an outage word, against that service's usual over the 7 days before. A surge is at least 5 headlines from at least 3 outlets and at least 5 times the usual. Blackout lists the outlets and links the articles; it does not reproduce their headlines.

    Both lanes count what people and outlets say. A company may deny an outage (Bank of America on 16 Nov 2026, Google of Gmail on 17 Nov 2026), and a surge can come from news of an outage elsewhere that names the company, a joke, or the words in another sense. Small outages often stay under both lines (Marriott and Gmail on 17 Nov 2026), and a service Bluesky's users and the press don't talk about stays quiet, so quiet here is not proof a service is up. Blackout does not assess a cause.

    Open incidents the providers report

    Incidents Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Apple, Cloudflare, Akamai, Slack, Zoom, GitHub, Starlink and Viasat list as open on their own status pages, as they report them.

    How this is measured

    Two separate checks. Network: IODA BGP and active probing on each watched network (ASN), median during the window against the median of the 7 days before; BO-IND-6 is a drop of ≥5% over ≥30 min (the largest 3-hour dip on these networks in the week to 12 Nov 2026 was 1.6%). Status feeds: incidents each provider lists as open on its own status page (BO-IND-7), republished as the provider reports them, with no assessment of cause. BO-IND-8: drops or incidents at two or more providers starting within 2 h.

    A status feed with nothing open is not proof a provider is up: Microsoft's public 365 page carries only the issues Microsoft posts there (smaller ones go only to each tenant's admin center), and Starlink's Statuspage has listed nothing since April 2022. Cloudflare's list keeps only incidents on its network, CDN, DNS (1.1.1.1 included), WARP or data centres, or rated major or critical. Viasat's page covers only its mobile satellite services (IP transport by region and push-to-talk). Akamai, Zoom, GitHub and Viasat are read from their 25-item incident history feeds, so an incident older than those would be missed; Akamai's security advisories are left out. Apple's items give the affected services, type and times only; Apple's own wording is on its page.

    Satellite internet traffic now

    Each satellite network's traffic, worldwide and in its top countries, as a percent of its own normal, lowest first. Satellite internet is shown here apart from cables and fibre.

    How this is measured

    From Cloudflare Radar's HTTP requests by network (ASN), every 4 hours, for each network in the register classed as satellite internet or as maritime and aviation satellite service. The level now is the mean of the last 6 full hours against the median, over the 28 days before, of the same hours of the day. Countries are where Radar places the network's requests (its top countries over 7 days, 0.5% of its requests or more: 20 for Starlink, 3 for the others). A network Radar sees no traffic from is listed as such, and a series Radar rounds to a few levels with many empty hours is named too thin to measure.

    The "same weekday" column is what the drop rule reads (BO-IND-21): the same 6 hours against the median of the same weekday's hours over the 4 weeks before, because satellite networks' traffic has a weekly shape (Viasat's falls to a fifth of its all-days normal every weekend). Under 70% of that on two runs in a row (8 h or more) makes a feed item, an observation and never an outage; after 3 days it is a standing condition. Series Radar rounds to a few levels (too thin), whose normal is under 20 counts an hour (too coarse: a 30% dip is then counting noise) or with fewer than 3 same weekdays on record are not judged, and say so there.

    Radar counts requests that reach Cloudflare, so Cloudflare's own trouble or a big holiday lowers every network at once. A reading is held, named here and not flagged, when all of Radar's traffic was under 85% of its normal in any hour of the drop, or when that control could not be read. For Starlink worldwide only, a short reading (BO-IND-22) flags any single hour of the last 6 under 60% of that hour's normal. When a country series is flagged, the row also says if all traffic from that country fell.

    IODA's view of each network the register marks confirmed: BGP and active probing over the last 3 hours against the same hours of the 7 days before. A signal with fewer than 20 measurements is too small to measure, as for countries.

    These are measurements of a network's own traffic, not of users, and no reading here is called an outage: a level under 100% can be a holiday, a sports final or a change in where the operator routes its traffic. Radar's estimated user counts are not used. Radar data CC BY-NC 4.0; IODA credited.

    Operators' own status reports are on the Providers tab: Starlink's Statuspage (operator unverified, and silent since April 2022) and Viasat's page for its mobile satellite services. HughesNet, Eutelsat OneWeb, Inmarsat, SES and Iridium publish no status page Blackout can read (checked 20 Nov 2026).

    How the series is built

    How Blackout judges

    Most items are measurements or reports, with no probability. Blackout makes one kind of judgment, with a probability, its ICD 203 likelihood term and a separate confidence: a cable-corridor outage, when drops coincide along a submarine cable corridor (BO-H-2A). A country outage is a measurement: it supports a shutdown (BO-H-1A) when OONI blocking rose in that country in the outage's own hours, and carries no probability until Verdict has scored that rule. An IODA drop alone fits a power or physical fault (BO-H-1B) as well as a shutdown. Provider items are the providers' own reports; Blackout never assesses their cause. Each item's doctrine detail (confidence, likelihood, competing hypotheses and how to tell them apart, National Critical Functions and sources) opens on the item itself.

    Collected about every 4 hours. A source that fails publishes nothing and is named in an "Incomplete collection" notice; nothing is filled in from another source. Times are stored in UTC and shown in your time zone (change it with the Time button).

    Satellite network traffic drops

    A network the register classes as satellite internet or as maritime and aviation service is flagged (BO-IND-21) when Cloudflare Radar's request volume from it, worldwide (Starlink also in each of its top 20 countries), is under 70% of its normal for the same weekday and hours on two runs in a row, 8 hours or more. The normal is the median, over the 4 weeks before, of the mean of the same 6 clock hours on the same weekday. The item is an observation of the network's own traffic, not of its users: a level under 100% can be a holiday, an event or a routing change, so it is never called an outage and no cause is assessed. After 3 days below the line it is a standing condition and drops to routine. A series too thin, too coarse or with fewer than 3 weeks of history is never flagged and is named on the Satellite tab. Radar data CC BY-NC 4.0.

    Held readings. Radar counts requests that reach Cloudflare, so a fault at Cloudflare or a major holiday lowers every network together. A reading does not flag when all of Radar's traffic worldwide was under 85% of its normal (the median of the same hour 1 to 4 weeks earlier) in any hour from the drop's start to now; it is named on the Satellite tab as held. If that control cannot be read, the reading is not flagged and the satellite lane lists the problem. A long drop that spans one such hour stays held until it ends.

    Short reading (BO-IND-22). For Starlink worldwide only, any one of the last 6 hours under 60% of the median of the same hour 1 to 4 weeks earlier, with at least 3 of those weeks and 100 Radar counts in the normal. It is the source's reading of the network's own traffic, is not called an outage, and gives no cause or likelihood. The 60% line was chosen on the same five known events it was tested on, so it still needs a check against later events. It is not used for countries or other networks, where the same line was mostly noise in the replay.

    Whole-country line. When a country series is flagged, all of that country's traffic over the same hours is read. If it is under 85% of normal, the item says so with the figure. That says the drop is not specific to the satellite network. It names no cause and never removes the item.

    Daily series for trends

    Each run also reads the last 60 days again from IODA's and Cloudflare Radar's own archives and publishes a count per country and UTC day in data/trends.json: IODA outage episodes (BGP, active-probing or darknet events, merged where they overlap), the hours of each day inside a BGP or active-probing event, Radar's annotated outages with the cause Cloudflare states, and Radar's verified traffic anomalies. Totals for the last 7, 14 and 30 complete days are set against the same number of days before. It counts what the sources report; it says how often and how long, not why. Compare a country with itself: IODA's BGP signal sees small networks drop out, so some countries show outages most days.

    IODA events of one signal that start in the same half hour in 20 or more countries are IODA's own collection, not the world's internet (it happened seven times in the 60 days to 20 Nov 2026), and are left out and listed in the file. A source that cannot be read leaves its counts empty, never zero. Days older than three days are kept for good in the repository's history/trends-days.jsonl.
